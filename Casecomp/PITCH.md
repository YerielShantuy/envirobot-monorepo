# The 3-minute pitch

### Speaker 1 — Recommendation · 0:00–0:26

> "Our plan: **Housing in Urban. Agriculture in Field. Water Treatment in Desert. Conservation in Rocky.**
>
> We scored all four zones against all four uses rather than ranking zones by moisture — that's the FAO land evaluation method. And one number anchors it: Urban, at 40 and 37 percent, is the only zone our sensor separates from every other zone with confidence. So it gets the use most sensitive to dry ground."

### Speaker 2 — Housing & Agriculture · 0:26–1:20

> "**Housing to Urban.** 40 and 37 — the driest ground we logged. AS 2870 classes residential sites by how far the surface moves as moisture cycles: driest zone, lowest class, cheapest compliant slab. WHO ties damp housing to **75 percent** higher respiratory risk. And it's infill — **56 thousand a lot against 150 thousand** greenfield.
>
> **Agriculture to Field.** 71 and 68. FAO is explicit: at field capacity air and water are ideal; at saturation there's no air, and most crops fail within **two to five days**. Field is our only farmable ground, and at 15 NTU its water is a minor clogging hazard."

### Speaker 3 — The inversion & Water Treatment · 1:20–2:12

> "**Rocky is 86 and 83** — the wettest zone, and that's exactly why it can't be farmed or built on. A 115-study meta-analysis puts average waterlogging loss at **33 percent**, **53 percent** past two weeks.
>
> But a *rocky* zone holding 86 percent is a perched wetland on rock. Wetlands are **6 percent** of the Earth's surface and hold **40 percent** of its species. And 71 NTU isn't pollution — ANZECC's lowland trigger is 6 to 50, so 71 says *investigate*; in the Lake Eyre Basin **11 of 14** natural sites exceed 100. **That's Conservation.**
>
> **Water Treatment goes to Desert** — buildable, no farm value, and the plant needs only 3 hectares."

### Speaker 4 — Trade-offs & caveats · 2:12–2:41

> "Four trade-offs. Urban puts 10,000 people in our cleanest catchment — the impervious cover model says degradation starts at **10 percent**, so a buffer and water-sensitive design are conditions, not extras. Field at 71 may need subsurface drainage, roughly **$3,000 to $5,000 a hectare**. Desert takes **76 years** to regrow perennial cover, so we cap the plant footprint. And Rocky locks up our largest water resource.
>
> Two honest caveats. These are **relative capacitive readings, not volumetric water content**. And every zone's two holes differ by exactly 3 points — so we treat each pair as one reading, not two independent samples."

# What we do NOT claim — say these before a judge does

These are the attacks that survived an adversarial review. Naming them first is worth more than pretending they don't exist.

1. **The soil scale is relative, not volumetric.** Capacitive sensors are reliable roughly **5–50% VWC**; all eight readings sit above that, so they must be read as a relative 0–100% scale between our dry and wet calibration points — not as VWC. We compare zones to each other, never to an absolute agronomic threshold.
2. **Only Urban is statistically separable.** At the sensor's stated **10–16% CV above 30% moisture**, Rocky/Field overlap by 0.5–9.6 points and Field/Desert by 1.8–9.5. Urban separates cleanly at both. So the confident claim is *"Urban is the driest"*; the Field-vs-Desert ordering is not resolvable and we don't lean on it.
3. **Every zone's two holes differ by exactly 3 points, second always lower** (40/37, 71/68, 86/83, 60/57). Real spatial variation doesn't do that. This is provided sample data with a uniform offset, so we treat each pair as **one reading with a known offset**, not two independent replicates.
4. **We have no elevation data.** EnviroBot measured moisture and turbidity only. Any gravity-fed-distribution argument would be an assumption, so **we don't make one** — the Desert siting rests on opportunity cost and buildability, not head.
5. **One turbidity grab sample per zone**, from an uncalibrated SEN0189 through a voltage divider, with no formazin standard. 6 vs 71 NTU is a real difference; **6 vs 15 is not resolvable** at this precision.
6. **The ADWG 5 NTU figure is for treated water at the tap** and <1 NTU is a disinfection-performance target — neither is a raw-source standard. We use them for relative ranking only.
7. **Turbidity says nothing about pathogens, nitrate or metals.** Before any intake decision we'd need E. coli, nutrients and metals.
8. **No flow direction or catchment boundary.** Turbidity runs 6 → 15 → 33 → 71 monotonically, which is also what one watercourse accumulating sediment downstream looks like. If these four zones are hydrologically connected, position matters and we'd re-plan around it.
9. **Two soil holes cannot characterise a whole quarter.** Assigning one land use per zone is a stronger assumption than averaging, not a weaker one.

**The one thing that would change the plan:** if Rocky's 86% turns out to be a rain event rather than a persistent water source, the wetland case falls. We'd re-log at 10, 40 and 100 cm after **14 dry days**, plus an EC reading in every hole — salinity biases these sensors **wet**, and that error runs in one direction only.

# Q&A

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **"Rocky has the most water. Why not farm it or put the plant there?"** | 86 and 83 is saturation, not abundance. FAO: at saturation there's no air in the soil and most crops fail in 2–5 days; the 115-study meta-analysis puts average waterlogging loss at 33%. And building there means excavating saturated ground on rock — the most expensive option we have. |
| **"71 NTU isn't healthy for an ecosystem."** | It isn't drinkable — that's 71× the ADWG disinfection target, which is why the intake isn't there. But for ecosystems there's no single number: ANZECC values are ecosystem- and region-specific and are compared to local reference condition. 6–50 NTU is the lowland trigger, so 71 says *investigate*, not *polluted*. In the Lake Eyre Basin 11 of 14 natural sites exceed 100 NTU. Our conservation case rests on the 86%, not on the water being clean. |
| **"Isn't Conservation just the zone you couldn't use?"** | We'd have a real case to use it — it holds the most water in the region and we're giving that up. We're conserving it because wetlands are 6% of the Earth's surface and 40% of its species, and because protecting a source catchment cuts downstream treatment cost about 20% for every 10% of natural cover. |
| **"Why site treatment on 33 NTU when 6 NTU is sitting in Urban?"** | Because the quarter hosts the works, not the intake — the brief says this quarter distributes region-wide and that quantity is sufficient everywhere. We concede 33 NTU needs conventional treatment with sedimentation rather than direct filtration. What we won't do is spend our only 67 hectares of dry, buildable ground on a 3-hectare plant. |
| **"How confident are you in these numbers?"** | Confident that Urban is the driest — it separates at both ends of the sensor's stated variability. Not confident in the Field-versus-Desert ordering; those overlap, and we don't build any argument on it. |
| **"Why not average the two holes?"** | We looked at the pairs and found every zone differs by exactly 3 points with the second always lower. That's not spatial variance, it's a uniform offset in the dataset — so averaging would invent precision. We report both and treat the pair as one reading. |
| **"What would change your plan?"** | If Rocky's 86% is a rain event, not a water table, Conservation moves to Desert and the plant moves to Rocky. Re-log at three depths after 14 dry days, with EC in every hole. |
| **"What's the 20-year risk?"** | Urban's impervious cover. Degradation begins above 10% and streams stop supporting aquatic life above 25%, and we've just put 10,000 people in our cleanest catchment. The buffer and water-sensitive urban design are conditions of the allocation, not nice-to-haves. |
